How Shamir's Secret Sharing works

The maths behind My Cloud's encryption, explained from scratch.

The idea in one sentence

A secret is split into \(n\) pieces called shares, handed out to different people, so that any \(k\) of them together can reconstruct the secret — but any \(k-1\) of them learn absolutely nothing about it. We call \(k\) the threshold. This is what makes it perfect for separation of duties: no single keyholder can decrypt a file alone; a quorum of \(k\) trusted parties must cooperate.

Crucially, the scheme is information-theoretically secure: with fewer than \(k\) shares the secret is not merely hard to compute (like breaking RSA), it is genuinely undetermined — every possible secret remains equally likely, even to an attacker with unlimited time and computing power.

The trick: points on a polynomial

The whole scheme rests on one fact from school algebra:

\(k\) points determine a unique polynomial of degree \(k-1\).

Two points determine a line (degree \(1\)); three points determine a parabola (degree \(2\)); and so on. Shamir's insight was to run this backwards:

  • Hide the secret \(S\) as the value of a polynomial at \(x = 0\).
  • Pick the other coefficients at random, giving a random curve of degree \(k-1\).
  • Hand out points on that curve as the shares.
Splitting the secret

To split a secret \(S\) with threshold \(k\), choose \(k-1\) random coefficients \(a_1, a_2, \dots, a_{k-1}\) and build the polynomial

\[ f(x) = S + a_1 x + a_2 x^2 + \dots + a_{k-1} x^{k-1} \]

Notice \(f(0) = S\): the secret is the y-intercept. The \(n\) shares are simply the points

\[ (1, f(1)),\ (2, f(2)),\ \dots,\ (n, f(n)) \]

Each person receives one point. In the figure below, the secret \(S\) sits on the y-axis and five shares are read off the same degree-2 curve (so here \(k = 3\)).

Recovering the secret

Bring any \(k\) shares back together and there is exactly one degree-\((k-1)\) curve through them. Reconstruct that curve and evaluate it at \(x = 0\) to read off the secret. The standard tool is Lagrange interpolation:

\[ f(x) = \sum_{j=1}^{k} y_j \prod_{\substack{1 \le m \le k \\ m \neq j}} \frac{x - x_m}{x_j - x_m} \]

We only ever need \(x = 0\), so the secret is

\[ S = f(0) = \sum_{j=1}^{k} y_j \prod_{\substack{1 \le m \le k \\ m \neq j}} \frac{-x_m}{x_j - x_m} \]

This is exactly what My Cloud computes when you supply your shares to decrypt a file.

Why fewer than \(k\) shares reveal nothing

Suppose \(k = 3\) but you hold only \(2\) shares. Two points fix a parabola with one degree of freedom left over — so there is a whole family of parabolas through your two points, and each one hits a different value at \(x = 0\). Every candidate secret is consistent with what you know, and they are all equally likely. Your two shares tell you nothing about which is the real \(S\).

Doing it with whole numbers: finite fields

Real-number arithmetic would leak information (the size and shape of the curve hint at the secret) and suffer rounding errors. So all the maths is done in a finite field — integers modulo a prime \(p\):

\[ f(x) = \Big( S + a_1 x + \dots + a_{k-1} x^{k-1} \Big) \bmod p \]

Every coefficient is a uniformly random element of \(\{0, 1, \dots, p-1\}\), which is what makes the "each secret equally likely" argument exact. Division in the Lagrange formula becomes multiplication by a modular inverse, found with the extended Euclidean algorithm.

My Cloud uses Mersenne primes \(p = 2^{q} - 1\) for this field, which is what the "field size exponent" \(q\) on the create-scheme form selects (\(q \in \{89, 107, 127\}\)). A larger \(q\) means a larger field and longer share strings, but does not change how many shares you need.

How My Cloud uses it

When you create a scheme, My Cloud generates a random secret \(S\) and its \(n\) shares, and stores only a salted hash of \(S\) (never the shares). To encrypt a file it derives a symmetric key as \(\text{key} = \operatorname{SHA256}(S)\) — a full-entropy 256-bit key — and encrypts with Fernet (AES-128). To decrypt, you re-enter any \(k\) of your shares; the secret is reconstructed by interpolation, checked against the stored hash, and the key is re-derived on the fly. The shares themselves are never stored anywhere — lose more than \(n-k\) of them and the file is gone for good.

One honest boundary: the secret sharing is information-theoretically secure (any \(k-1\) shares reveal nothing, even to a quantum computer), but the file cipher (AES) is only computationally secure. Hashing the secret with SHA-256 makes that layer a clean AES-128 key so its strength doesn't depend on the secret's decimal length.
